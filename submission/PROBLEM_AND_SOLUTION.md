# Problem and solution

## The learning problem

The official [challenge Background](https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/2026/challenges/space-mission-design-game/) describes how mission objectives, spacecraft, instruments, orbit, launch, resources and operations interact, while students rarely experiment with the entire process. A better camera is not an isolated upgrade: it can need more mass, power, data capacity and money.

## MissionX's response

MissionX lets a player make and revise those decisions within a bounded educational catalog. The player starts with a science goal, assembles compatible components, sees live resource consequences, fixes blocking checks, and launches a repeatable seeded scenario. Received data, energy/memory constraints, final requirements and report explanations make the outcome inspectable instead of merely showing a cinematic success screen.

Three missions expose different authored constraints. Sun Watch uses four identical craft observing the Sun from Earth orbit; its fleet totals and per-craft power/battery budgets are labeled separately. Reusing a design with a different seed demonstrates that configuration readiness is not a launch guarantee.

## Value and evidence boundaries

The strength is a connected, playable explanation of trade-offs with local replay/reporting, not an aerospace optimizer. The current project is single-player; collaboration/multiplayer suggested by the challenge is not implemented. No classroom trial, measured comprehension improvement, user-count claim, novelty-first claim or professional mission validation is established. The team can demonstrate functionality and invite feedback without claiming proven educational impact.
